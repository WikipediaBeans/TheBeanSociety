The Baked Bean Society is a British comedy-drama television series which was broadcast on BBC Two for six episodes in 1996. It starred Dan as Dan, an underachieving tennis coach whose devotion to baked beans repeatedly intrudes upon his attempts to run a small social club in suburban Hertfordshire.

Conceived as an offbeat study of male friendship, local politics and tinned food, the programme received hostile contemporary reviews. Its final episodes introduced Julian, a character intended to introduce the British public to a flamboyantly gay lifestyle; the portrayal was widely criticised as ill-judged. Protests and subsequent legal inquiries followed, and the series was cancelled after its first run. It subsequently acquired a small cult following, particularly after repeat screenings on late-night television in the early 2000s.[1]

Premise

Dan is an enthusiastic but chronically unsuccessful tennis instructor who inherits the keys to the Baked Bean Society, a community hall founded by his late uncle. Against the advice of the committee, Dan attempts to revive the society through a series of increasingly impractical bean-themed events, while pursuing a place in the county veterans' tennis league. In the final two episodes, Julian arrives in a hired white limousine with a stuffed swan and appoints himself the hall's "new events adviser", an introduction that shifted the programme's tone abruptly.

Cast

  • Dan as Dan
  • Julian as Julian, the society's new events adviser
  • Moira Keene as Sheila Wren, the society's treasurer
  • Peter Lomas as Roy Clegg, Dan's doubles partner
  • Harriet Finch as Pam, Dan's sister

Production

The series was filmed in and around St Albans during the autumn of 1995. Its working title was Beans on the Baseline; the final title was chosen after a focus group objected that the original "sounded like a cookery programme with rackets".[2]

Reception and legacy

Reviewers were largely dismissive. The Evening Ledger described it as "a programme that mistakes repetition for charm", while another review questioned whether its extended scenes of Dan arranging tins had been intended as comedy.[3]

The introduction of Julian was received particularly badly. Although the producers said the character was intended as an affirming and flamboyant representation of gay life, critics and viewers objected to the writing and its treatment of the subject, including a scene in which Julian attempts to open a nightclub inside a filing cabinet. Protests outside Broadcasting House and legal inquiries into the complaints followed; BBC Two withdrew plans for a second series.

Despite this, the programme gained admirers for Dan's sincerely committed performance and its peculiar, low-key atmosphere. The final episode, "Tie-break at the Fayre", has become a favourite among fans for Dan's unsuccessful attempt to serve a tin of beans across a grass court.
